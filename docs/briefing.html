The signature protects the QR code and nothing else

An Aadhaar card has two parts and only one of them is protected. Every consequence below follows from that single fact.

How a forger passes the system as it stands

  1. Photograph a stranger's genuine Aadhaar — the QR on the back alone. It is signed by UIDAI, and copying it does not change that.
  2. Print a fresh card carrying your own face and name on the front and their genuine QR on the back.
  3. The system verifies the QR. The signature is valid, because it truly is UIDAI's. Verdict: GENUINE_SIGNED.
  4. The system then compares the live selfie against the photograph on the front — your own face, because you printed it. Binding: BOUND.
  5. Every check has passed. The card is a forgery.

“Can't a forger simply copy a real QR code?” is the likeliest question we will be asked, because it is the obvious objection to the entire idea. Today the honest answer is yes, and the system reports it genuine.

2settled

We sign our own cards

Verifying real Aadhaar cards requires UIDAI's production certificate, which is a procurement errand, and real people's identity documents, which brings consent and privacy obligations into an internal round for no gain in technical merit. We operate our own certificate authority instead.

This is not a staged demonstration. The payload format, the RSA-2048 signature, the SHA-256 digest, the field framing and the embedded photograph are exactly what UIDAI emits; only the signing key differs. The verification code runs its ordinary path with nothing stubbed or bypassed. Substitute UIDAI's certificate and the same code verifies real cards — which is a stronger thing to say to an examiner than any excuse for not having one.

Section 2Order of
work

Ordered so that stopping anywhere still leaves a demonstration

Nothing lower begins while anything higher is open. If time runs out mid-list, everything above the cut still forms something coherent to show.

First — make the claims true (about a day)

Match the selfie against the signed photograph, and check the printed name and number against the signed fields; this closes Finding 1 and converts our hardest question into our best answer. Stop a valid signature from suppressing a failed checksum — one elif in the verdict engine. Write the marksheet total check, which is deterministic, explainable, and the moment we point at when asked to catch a forgery live. Make redaction actually mask the name, date of birth and address, since that is our own stated constraint.

Second — stop accusing honest documents (half a day)

Raise the ELA threshold above its own noise floor and never let it raise a flag alone. Mark it inapplicable to photographs of printouts, because printing destroys the very evidence the method depends upon. Saying so plainly is a strength; a false accusation costs an honest person far more than a missed forgery costs us.

Third — the face people see (remaining time)

A React console showing the verdict, the reasons in plain language, and the history. Then rehearse the four demonstration steps against generated specimens, so that nothing is seen for the first time in front of an audience.

Section 3Whose problem
this is

Three people stand around one document, and all three lose

Every technical decision above should trace to somebody's actual difficulty. Before more is built, it is worth being exact about whose.

The verifier — a recruiter screening a candidate, an admissions desk, a bank's compliance officer. They are handed a photocopy and have no means of telling it from a competent forgery, so they glance at it and absorb the risk. Verifying properly with the issuing university takes weeks, costs money, and frequently receives no reply.

The holder — an applicant, a student, someone claiming a scheme they are entitled to. Their papers are genuine, yet they wait to be believed, submit the same documents again to every new institution, and can be turned away over a poor scan or a faded print. They carry the cost of a system that cannot distinguish truth from forgery.

The issuer — a university or board answering verification requests by telephone and email, one at a time, by hand. They have no way to demonstrate at scale what they did and did not issue, while forged versions of their certificates circulate.

The verifier's difficulty and the holder's difficulty are one problem seen from two sides, and they pull against each other. Tuned to catch every forgery, a system wrongly flags honest people with imperfect paperwork; tuned never to offend, it catches nothing.

This is precisely why the verdict ladder exists, and why it is the product rather than a technicality. Separating this is forged from we could not confirm this means the second answer sends a person to a human being instead of rejecting them.

It also shows where to aim next. The holder's worst difficulty — proving the same genuine documents over and over — is not addressed by better forgery detection at all. It is addressed by documents that can prove themselves: signed once at source, checkable by anyone, indefinitely. That is the argument for issuer-side signing, and a register of issuance and revocation is the one place a ledger genuinely earns its keep, because a signature can prove what was issued but cannot withdraw it.

Section 4Position

What can honestly be claimed

Without something cryptographic to check, software can detect some forgeries but can never establish authenticity. That is a limit of the problem, not of our effort or our skill, and stating it plainly is worth more than any accuracy figure we could invent.

Where we can genuinely go further than existing practice is the camera. Ordinary know-your-customer checks verify the credential and then trust whatever the lens is shown. Our own liveness test is a blink check, defeated by playing a video on a telephone screen. Detecting screen replay and presentation attacks is a real contribution and the strongest honest answer to why this is better than what already exists.

We verify the credential cryptographically, bind it to a living person, and are candid about the documents where cryptography is unavailable.

Everything in this report exists so that sentence can be said truthfully.
